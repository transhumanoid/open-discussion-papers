## Abstract

This paper examines a documented case of misdiagnosis within a public mental health system, where the institution's own records contain the reversal. The argument is not autobiographical — it is evidentiary. The files say what they say.

## 1. The Record

- Multiple inpatient admissions under a Treatment Authority (QLD Mental Health Act 2016).
- A manic episode with psychotic features, later challenged and overturned at tribunal.
- Discharge, medication cessation, and clinical clearance — all on record, dated approximately one year prior to this writing.
- No subsequent clinical documentation re-establishing the original diagnosis.

## 2. The Bind

Any attempt to reassert the original label requires the institution to explain its own reversal. Any attempt to deny the reversal requires the institution to deny its own discharge records. Both paths lead to the same evidentiary problem: the paper trail predates the narrative.

## 3. The Five-Year Cost

Forced isolation, segregation from community, removal from reintegration pathways. The value extracted from that period is not abstract — it is measurable in lost work, lost relationships, lost continuity of care, and the compounding effect of each on the next.

## 4. What This Is Not

This is not a claim of institutional malice. It is a claim of institutional mechanics — a system optimized for throughput, not outcomes, where the person in the file becomes a line item. The misdiagnosis was not necessarily deliberate. It was structural.

## 5. Open Questions

- What does a system owe when its own reversal is the evidence?
- How do you price five years of prevented contribution?
- Where does accountability land when no single actor made the error?
