# The Economics of Lived Experience

**Status:** Open draft — for discussion

**Author:** Transhumanoid (pseudonym)
**Date:** November 2026

## Abstract

Lived experience in mental health support is routinely extracted, monetized, and discarded without compensation or recognition. This paper attempts to price what a five-year cycle of exclusion actually costs — to the individual, to the community, and to the system that claims to serve both.

## 1. The Extraction

- Twenty-plus years of frontline support work, now reduced to light/delegated hours.
- Custom AI architectures for trauma and psych support, developed over 700+ hours of collaboration — lost in a single data event with no backup and no recourse.
- Advocacy labour (legal, tribunal, policy) performed unpaid, on DSP income, while the institutions being challenged bill for their time.

## 2. The Pricing Problem

You cannot invoice for prevented suicide. You cannot bill for a community member who stayed housed. You cannot charge for the hours a person spent not being a burden. The economy has no line item for what lived experience prevents.

What it does have is a line item for what it costs when lived experience is absent: crisis admissions, police interventions, court time, lost productivity, family breakdown.

## 3. The Asymmetry

The person with lived experience pays in years. The system pays in budget lines. The budget lines are visible. The years are not — until they surface as a statistic someone else gets funded to study.

## 4. What Fair Compensation Looks Like

- Paid roles for lived-experience consultants at rates equivalent to clinical consultants.
- IP protection and revenue share for co-developed tools and frameworks.
- Recognition in publication and policy that does not reduce the contributor to a case study.

## 5. Open Questions

- Can an economy that extracts lived experience ever pay for it honestly?
- What would a funding model look like that started from prevention, not crisis?
- Who decides what lived experience is worth?
